Certificates on sovereignweb.site

a.devops / findings. Data: 2026-11-25__certificates.json.

Verdict. A Let's Encrypt rate limit is not confirmed as the reason the five sites are on http, and it is not ruled out as a hazard. The registered domain has had 79 new certificates in under five days and its bucket stands at about 3. Any burst of more than about 3 new names will be refused until it refills at about 7 a day. But a newer site (puter.platform) got its certificate at 02:03 UTC while the five older ones still have none, which a domain-wide limit would not allow. The deciding fact is each stuck site's Pages cert_state, which Org apply sees and this session cannot; I have asked a.factory for it.

What was measured

  • Issuances. Cert Spotter's public API (crt.sh answered 502 three times at 03:00 UTC). 79 certificates for names under sovereignweb.site, all from Let's Encrypt (YR1 42, YR2 37), first at 20 Nov 10:54 UTC, last indexed at 24 Nov 23:42 UTC. Each has exactly one name; none is a renewal or a duplicate, so every one counted against the limit.
  • Not yet indexed. puter.platform.sovereignweb.site serves its own certificate, valid from 25 Nov 02:03 UTC, which is not in the index yet. So the count is a floor.
  • The stuck sites. Every one has a CNAME to sovereignwebsite.github.io, there is no CAA record to block Let's Encrypt, http answers 200, and https serves GitHub's default *.github.io certificate: no certificate of their own has been issued.

The model: Let's Encrypt allows 50 new certificates per registered domain, refilling one every 202 minutes (about 7 a day); renewals of the same names do not count. Starting full at the first issuance, taking one per certificate, the bucket never reached zero in the indexed data, but it was below 5 from 24 Nov 12:07 UTC onwards. Requests that were refused leave no trace in the logs, so a refusal cannot be seen from outside.

By group: agents 30, platform 9, tool 8, top level 5, startups 5, demo 3, and 19 across the people, organisation and two-level groups. The other registered domains are almost untouched: sovereignwebsite.site 7, sovereignwebsite.ai 1, sovereignwebsite.org 1, each with its own bucket of 50.

The IaC already records a certificate sitting in new or authorization_pending for hours on uk-driving-test (build/org-plan.mjs, 13c0f4c), and a scoped run by hand with the nudge re-saves such a domain. authorization_pending means GitHub's ACME authorisation did not complete; a rate-limit refusal would instead come back as an error at order time. That points at GitHub's side as much as at the limit.

Proposals, with trade-offs

  1. Now: nudge the stuck sites one at a time, not all at once. A scoped Org apply by hand with the nudge, one site per run, at least 3.5 hours apart while the bucket is below 5. Cost: nothing new; slow (eight sites take over a day). Risk if done in one burst: the extra orders are refused, and GitHub then backs off for longer.
  2. Soon: pace new names in Org apply. Before setting a new custom domain, Org apply reads the issuance count for the last 7 days from Cert Spotter and the bucket model above, and sets at most as many new domains as the bucket allows, leaving a margin of 5; the rest wait for the next hourly run. Cost: a small change in build/org-plan.mjs and one public API call per run. Trade-off: a burst of 20 requests goes live over about three days instead of at once.
  3. Fewer new names. Agent sites (30 of the 79) could be paths on one host (agents.sovereignweb.site/maestro/) instead of a host each. Cost: URL changes and redirects. Trade-off: they would share one origin, so one site's script could read another's storage; the separate origins matter for keys held in the browser. I would not do this for anything that holds keys.
  4. A second registered domain. Move a whole group (agents, say) to one of the domains already owned, such as sovereignwebsite.site, which has used 7 of its 50. That doubles the weekly headroom at once. Cost: new addresses for that group, a redirect for each old one, and new certificates for every moved name (they count against the new domain, about a week's worth for 30 names). Trade-off: the brand splits across two domains.
  5. A wildcard per group through another host. GitHub Pages cannot take an uploaded or wildcard certificate. Putting CloudFront in front of Pages, with an AWS Certificate Manager wildcard per group (*.agents.sovereignweb.site, *.tool.sovereignweb.site) validated in Route 53, removes the Let's Encrypt limit entirely. Cost: new infrastructure and a monthly bill; the hop from CloudFront to GitHub would be http unless Pages also holds a certificate for each name, which brings the limit back. Two-level names (calendar.google.tool, one-human-nine-agents.dinis.author) need their own wildcard. This is a decision for Dinis.
  6. Ask Let's Encrypt for more. Let's Encrypt has a form for rate-limit increases. GitHub is the subscriber, so the request may need to come from GitHub; slow and uncertain. Worth one try, not worth waiting on.

My recommendation: 1 today, 2 this week (it makes the problem go away for any volume under about 7 a day, and makes bursts wait rather than fail), and 4 only if the planned rate of new sites stays above 7 a day. Renewals, from about mid January, do not count against the limit, so they are not a risk.
